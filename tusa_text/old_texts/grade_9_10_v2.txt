By the mid-eighteenth century, the population of Great Britain's North American colonies was varied. Twenty percent of the population was African, and a third of the white population came from lands other than England. Each of those peoples brought their own language, customs, values, and beliefs, their own culture. Each, not surprisingly, hoped to continue their familiar ways of life in their new homeland. Even so, by the mid-1700s the stamp of England was fixed on most of North America. English was the language of the government and of two-thirds of the people. Settlers lived under the laws of England as well as under local governments modeled on England's government. Thus English ideas about society prevailed in the colonies. Among the most important English ideas was a cluster of beliefs, traditions, and institutions about the rights of individuals and government. These included the right to own property, the right to be represented in the government that made the laws one lived under, and the right to be protected against unfair and unreasonable treatment by government. Individuals could not be arrested or imprisoned without being charged with an offense. They were also entitled to a speedy trial by jury. The English men and women who settled America, no less than those who stayed in England, regarded those ""rights of Englishmen"" as their birthright and guarded them jealously. In addition to the ideas about the individual and government, there was a group of English ideas and institutions that concerned the individual and society. One was the institution of an established church, an official religion that all inhabitants, regardless of their religious beliefs, supported by taxes. Another was the division of society into ranks, or classes. The ranks were determined by birth and wealth.
